Grades 7–12 · Project-Based Civics

Food System Civic  Engagement  Project

Empower your secondary students to become real changemakers in their local food system — through a year-long, project-based civics initiative.

About the Project

The Food System Civic Engagement Project is a project-based initiative that invites a select cohort of secondary educators (grades 7–12), students, and community partners to take a deep dive into how food connects to civic engagement, sustainability, and social justice. From September through May, participating educators gain background knowledge and resources to support student-driven community civics projects that tackle real issues in their local food system. Participating educators will:

  • Support students to design and lead a civics project addressing real local food challenges
  • Access research tools, resources, and individualized support
  • Build connections with educator peers and mentors across Massachusetts
  • Connect food systems learning to civics, sustainability, and social justice standards

Student-Led Projects

Projects included building pollinator flower boxes in the community, building connections between a school and a food pantry, teaching the community the impact of climate change and flooding on local farmers, building a campaign to promote sustainable seafood, and reducing food waste in the school cafeteria.

“ From a participating educator

Several groups of students created presentations about food waste and its environmental impact that were delivered to some seventh-grade classrooms. These student groups did this because they identified a root cause of food waste as simply a lack of education about food waste and its harmful effects. Another group of students conducted a food waste audit in our cafeteria during lunch one day. They collected data and calculated totals, and, when everything was said and done, we learned that roughly 36.5 pounds of food is wasted in our cafeteria daily. In a school year, this adds up to 6,570 pounds, which is roughly the same weight as a hippopotamus! Lastly, there were also some student groups that really wanted to focus on composting. These groups created presentations that were delivered to our school administrators. These groups also put together a composting proposal in which they researched three different composting companies and reported the pricing, pros, and cons to our administrators as well. Our principal is committed to starting a small composting program next year, and then increasing its prevalence in our building over the course of the next four years….One of the best experiences occurred when students reached out to the cafeteria manager at our school, as well as our district food services manager. When students began reaching out to them, these individuals decided to take action on their own to try to reduce food waste in our school. They committed to sitting down over the summer to review the menu for next year in an effort to trim down portion sizes (within reason) so there is less food waste in the future. In addition, these two individuals also created their own survey that they sent out to the entire school to get feedback/suggestions for the menu for next year's lunch. When these actions occurred, I shared with students that this phenomenon - when you are able to inspire others to take their own action on an issue you are passionate about - is one mark of a successful civics action project. Through this, students learned that their voice is powerful and can be used to enact change in their own community.
