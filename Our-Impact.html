About Massachusetts Farm to School

Our Impact

Local food in schools pays off for students, for producers, and for whole communities. Here is what that looks like across Massachusetts.

01

What it does for students

Local food on the lunch tray. Hands-on learning in the classroom and the garden. Students who know what's in season, where it grew, and the names of the people who grew it — and who carry that into the rest of their lives.

02

What it does for producers

Massachusetts farmers, fishers, food processors, and ranchers gain access to an institutional market that buys regularly, in volume, year after year. School food service is one of the most reliable institutional buyers in the food system, and farm-to-school is how local producers compete for that business.

03

What it does for communities

Federal school meal dollars stay in our local economy instead of leaving the state. Parents see what their kids eat. Teachers, food directors, farmers, and families end up around the same table. New jobs follow.

Massachusetts by the Numbers

68% of MA school districts report participating in farm-to-school activities.
$10,262,200 invested in local food across Massachusetts schools.
25% of MA farmers report increasing production to meet institutional demand.
At least 210 school gardens growing across the Commonwealth.

Why It Matters

For health

Healthy eating habits are easier to start in kindergarten than to fix in high school. Better food on the tray means kids actually eat their lunch — and that food security extends to families through the rest of the day.

For learning

Kids who eat well learn better. School gardens, classroom lessons, and on-farm field trips work for the experiential learners who don't always thrive in a lecture-and-worksheet classroom. And fresher food simply tastes better — kids eat more of it.

For the economy

Every cafeteria order is a business contract for a Massachusetts farmer. School meal programs run healthier financially when more kids choose to eat lunch. Federal dollars circulate in our state instead of leaving it.

For the environment

Local land stays in farming. Shorter trips from field to fork mean lower emissions. School-side composting and farm-side gleaning cut food waste at both ends.

The research base for farm-to-school is real and still growing.
